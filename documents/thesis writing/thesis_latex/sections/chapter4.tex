These figures are point estimates from the single evaluation pass described in Section~\ref{sec:m_evaluation}, and are not accompanied here by a bootstrap confidence interval: resampling the test split to attach an interval to each cell of Table~\ref{tab:testresults} was not completed within the scope of this dissertation. The defect row is the one most affected by this gap, since it rests on 66 instances against 149--191 for each ripening class, and its reported figures should be read with correspondingly wider uncertainty in mind; this is recorded as a limitation in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} rather than left unstated.

Two patterns in Table~\ref{tab:testresults} deserve comment.

The three classes at the ends of the colour progression, green and red, are detected most reliably, at mAP@50 of 0.982 and 0.988. The classes in the middle of the progression perform less well, and the defect class least well of all. This ordering is not incidental. Green and red are defined by the absence and the near-completeness of red colouration respectively, which are absolute rather than relative judgements, whereas breaker and turning are separated from their neighbours by a threshold on a continuous quantity. A boundary placed at 30\,\% or 80\,\% red surface has no visual discontinuity behind it, so fruit close to either boundary is genuinely ambiguous rather than merely difficult.

The gap between mAP@50 and mAP@50--95 across all classes, from 0.936 to 0.711 overall, indicates that the model finds fruit reliably but localises the box less tightly. For this application that ordering is the favourable one: the sorting decision depends on the class label and on the fruit being detected at all, not on the precision of the box edges.

The raw confusion matrix is built at a fixed, permissive confidence threshold rather than the confidence-swept operating point behind Table~\ref{tab:testresults}, so the large background row and column in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} reflect low-confidence candidate boxes that never reach the deployment threshold of 0.45, not the model's deployed error rate; the pattern worth reading from this figure is therefore the relative size of the class-to-class cells, not their absolute counts. Read that way, the two largest off-diagonal masses are between green and breaker (82 true-green fruit predicted breaker, plus 58 true-breaker fruit predicted green) and between breaker and turning (50 true-turning fruit predicted breaker, plus 45 true-breaker fruit predicted turning), with turning and red not far behind (52 and 36 in the two directions). Every one of these is a pair of classes adjacent to each other on the ripening progression. By contrast, the defect class exchanges only a handful of instances with any ripening class, at most 7, against a green--breaker exchange of 140. The dominant confusion in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} is therefore between adjacent ripening classes on the continuous colour gradient, not with the defect class, which is exactly the pattern the per-class mAP figures in Table~\ref{tab:testresults} already show.

Training converged in 4.57~hours. Early stopping halted the run after epoch~77, restoring the checkpoint from epoch~52 as the final model. The fused model comprises 93 layers and 25{,}842{,}655 parameters at 78.7~GFLOPs, and processes a test image in 6.7~ms of inference time, with 0.7~ms of preprocessing and 0.7~ms of postprocessing, on the RTX 3070~Ti host. At 30~\acrshort{FPS} the camera delivers a frame every 33~ms, so inference is not the limiting factor in the real-time path.

Figure~\ref{fig:prcurves} is also the basis of the deployment confidence threshold of 0.45 used in Section~\ref{sec:m_realtime}, taken as the approximate maximum of the $F_1$ curve.

The two architectures are effectively tied on detection rate: the mAP@50 difference of $-0.006$ overall is within the range that would be expected from run-to-run variation on a test split of this size. Localisation quality differs clearly and consistently, with YOLO26m ahead on mAP@50--95 in every class and by 0.074 overall.

The exception is instructive. The defect class is the only class where YOLO26m loses ground on detection rate, falling from 0.804 to 0.754, while simultaneously gaining 0.082 on mAP@50--95. The class that improves least in detection is the same class that was reconstructed after the confound described in Section~\ref{sec:annotation_qc}, has the fewest real training instances, and relies most heavily on the augmentation supplement. That is consistent with the defect class remaining the most fragile part of the dataset rather than with any deficiency of the architecture.

YOLOv8m was retained for deployment. The metric on which YOLO26m leads, mAP@50--95, rewards tight box regression, which the sorting mechanism does not use: the gate decision depends on the class label and on the fruit being detected, not on box edge precision. On the metrics that do bear on sorting, detection rate and defect-class recall, YOLOv8m is equal or ahead. This is a task-specific justification rather than a claim that one architecture is generally superior.

A formal bootstrap treatment of these differences, of the kind described in Section~\ref{sec:m_evaluation}, has not yet been computed for this comparison. The interpretation above is therefore offered qualitatively, from the size of each difference relative to the test split, rather than from resampled confidence intervals. Attaching those intervals to Table~\ref{tab:archcomparison} remains outstanding and should be completed before the mAP@50 differences in particular are presented as more than run-to-run noise.

The uncontrolled differences between the two training runs, noted in Section~\ref{sec:m_experiments}, must be carried into the interpretation. The comparison model was trained with a fixed batch size of two against automatic batch selection for the deployed model, and the runs early-stopped after different numbers of epochs. The result therefore establishes which of two concretely trained models better suits this task, and not which architecture is superior under matched optimisation budgets.

\subsection{Effect of the Dataset Corrections}
\label{sec:r_corrections}

Three successive versions of the model were trained as the two corrections of Section~\ref{sec:annotation_qc} were applied in turn. Defect-class and overall mAP@50 for each version are given in Table~\ref{tab:corrections}.

\begin{table}[H]
\caption{Defect class and overall detection performance across successive dataset corrections}
\label{tab:corrections}
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{p{4.6cm} p{3.0cm} r r}
\toprule
\textbf{Correction stage} & \textbf{Dataset version} & \textbf{Defect mAP@50} & \textbf{Overall mAP@50} \\
\midrule
Before correction (publicly sourced defect images, mixed polygon/box annotation, uncontrolled background) & Initial import & 0.595 & 0.903 \\
After the box-geometry fix (2026-07-25) & tomato\_5class\_local & 0.940 & 0.962 \\
After the imaging-conditions fix (2026-07-29), current deployed model & tomato\_5class\_v6\_balanced & 0.804 & 0.936 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The first correction alone appears to fix the problem: defect mAP@50 rises from 0.595 to 0.940 once every defect image is re-annotated to the whole-fruit box convention. That figure is misleading. At that point the defect class was still drawn largely from the publicly sourced images, which differ from the four ripening classes in background and lighting as well as in box convention. A held-out test split drawn from the same source distribution rewards exactly that difference, so a high offline score at this stage reflects the model separating defect images by their capture conditions, not by damage. This is precisely the confound identified only once live camera testing began (Section~\ref{sec:annotation_qc}), and it is not visible in Table~\ref{tab:corrections} alone.

The second correction, removing the mismatched public images and recapturing defect fruit under the same lightbox conditions as the other four classes, is the one that fixes the underlying problem, and it is also the one that makes the offline number fall, to 0.804. The post-correction test split is smaller (66 instances against a larger pool drawn partly from the public source in the previous version) and harder, because the defect class can no longer be separated by background or lighting and must be distinguished by the fruit itself. The drop from 0.940 to 0.804 is therefore not a regression in the ordinary sense: it is the offline metric becoming an honest measure of the harder, correctly posed problem, at the cost of losing the artificially inflated score the confound had been providing. The evidence that the correction worked is not this table by itself but the live classification behaviour reported in Section~\ref{sec:r_live}, where the same fix removes a failure mode this offline comparison cannot see.

A full per-class precision, recall and mAP@50--95 breakdown is not available for the intermediate \texttt{tomato\_5class\_local} run: only the mAP@50 values above were retained from that evaluation, and the more detailed metrics were not logged at the time. This general lesson, that a scarce class topped up from an outside source can teach a network the capture conditions instead of the object, and that fixing one confound can unmask a second that offline metrics alone conceal, applies well beyond tomato grading.

On 2026-07-26, defect accounted for 39.2\,\% of all classifications made, at a mean confidence of 0.63; screenshot evidence from that session (\texttt{live dashboard results-2026.07.26/}) shows healthy red, green and turning fruit being tagged defect, consistent with the confound described in Section~\ref{sec:annotation_qc}. Because neither session recorded a ground-truth label against each individual classification, this comparison cannot be reported as a per-class or overall accuracy percentage, and it is not presented as one. A separate observation from the 2026-07-29 screenshots is relevant to the non-tomato object rejection test (Section~\ref{sec:r_tests}, T-05): at least three non-tomato objects visible in that session's screenshots, a bottle cap, a mobile phone and a wristwatch, were classified into a tomato class at confidences between 50\,\% and 81\,\%, rather than being rejected. Whether the non-tomato filtering stage of Section~\ref{sec:m_realtime} was active during this particular session was not recorded, so this is reported as an anecdotal observation rather than a controlled test result.

\subsection{Conveyor Transport and End-to-End Sorting}
\label{sec:r_sorting}

Experiment 4 was planned in two parts, and only the first has been carried out.

\textbf{Mechanical transport.} The assembled conveyor was run with fresh fruit on the belt, with no vision or sorting logic active. The trial confirmed that the conveyor transported tomatoes smoothly, at consistent speed and without stalling or displacement. The number of fruits run and the belt speed in centimetres per second were not recorded at the time of this trial, so the timing implications for the gate release delay of Section~\ref{sec:m_integration} cannot yet be quantified from this result; the outcome establishes that the mechanical transport itself is fit for purpose, without establishing the numbers needed to interpret gate timing precisely.

\textbf{Closed-loop end-to-end sorting.} This part of the experiment, in which classifications from the live pipeline would be converted into gate commands and the resulting physical diversions checked against the fruit's known class, has not been carried out. At the time of writing, the gate servo angles have not been validated against the assembled hardware, and the belt-speed and gate-spacing constants used by the timing logic remain unmeasured placeholders rather than figures taken from the running rig. No per-class diversion count, correct-gate rate or failure-mode breakdown can therefore be reported for this experiment. Completing this trial is the single largest remaining gap in the evidence for the system as a whole, since it is the only test that exercises detection, tracking, the command protocol and gate actuation together on physical fruit.

This should be treated as unresolved: if the underlying record is subsequently located and it supports these values independently, the exact agreement with \citet{abekoon2024} would be a genuinely notable result and should be stated as such; if no such record can be produced, Table~\ref{tab:shelfresults} should be described as adapted from the published reference rather than measured directly, and the remaining-shelf-life values used elsewhere in this dissertation inherit the same caveat.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:r_discussion}

\textbf{Feasibility of cultivar-specific grading.} An overall mAP@50 of 0.936 and precision and recall both above 0.90 (Table~\ref{tab:testresults}) show that a detector fine-tuned on a purpose-built Padma dataset can localise and classify this cultivar's fruit reliably from a held-out split, at a speed the real-time path does not bottleneck on (Section~\ref{sec:r_test}). The published YOLO-based tomato ripeness work reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:detection} reports comparably high figures for other cultivars and imaging setups, so this result is best read as confirming that the same architecture family transfers to Padma once trained on Padma-specific data, rather than as a novel level of accuracy; the contribution of this study, as scoped in Section~\ref{sec:gaps}, is the cultivar-specific dataset and the conveyor-integrated system built around it, not a new state of the art on the detection metric alone.

\textbf{The ripening-boundary confusion is a property of the class definition.} Green and red, the two classes defined by an absolute judgement, red surface entirely absent or entirely present, are detected most reliably (mAP@50 of 0.982 and 0.988). Breaker and turning, defined by a threshold on a continuous quantity, are detected less reliably, and the defect class, which competes for training data and, historically, for capture conditions, less reliably still (Section~\ref{sec:r_test}). This ordering is exactly what a hard multi-class boundary imposed on a continuous colour gradient predicts, and it recurs throughout the results: it is present in the single-model test split, and it recurs as the class most sensitive to the dataset corrections in Section~\ref{sec:r_corrections}. Anyone defining discrete classes on a continuous physical quantity should expect the same pattern, and the implication, developed further in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}, is that an ordinal or regression-based formulation would match the underlying variable more closely than a flat multi-way classifier.

\textbf{The sensor-released gate design.} Section~\ref{sec:m_integration} argues that releasing each gate from its own presence sensor, rather than from a delay computed from belt speed and camera-to-gate distance as in Equation~\eqref{eq:timed}, removes the dominant, distance-accumulating source of timing error. That argument is an engineering justification, not yet a demonstrated result: the mechanical transport trial (Section~\ref{sec:r_sorting}) confirms the belt itself runs smoothly, but the closed-loop trial that would show the sensor-released design correctly diverting fruit to the intended gate has not been carried out. The design as implemented is sound on the reasoning given in Section~\ref{sec:actuation}, but until Experiment 4's second part is completed, its advantage over the timed alternative is argued rather than measured.

\textbf{Shelf life compared with the published Padma study.} The stage durations currently on record for this project's observation study match the published Padma values of \citet{abekoon2024} exactly once mapped through the four-class correspondence of Table~\ref{tab:merge} (Section~\ref{sec:r_shelflife}). As discussed there, an agreement this precise more plausibly reflects the published figures having been carried into this project's records than an independent 100-fruit replication, and this must be resolved against the project's own raw observation data before it is presented either as a striking confirmation of \citet{abekoon2024} or, if no independent record exists, disclosed as an adapted rather than measured value.

\textbf{What the corrective cycle implies for dataset construction practice.} The most transferable finding in this chapter is not a single number but the shape of the correction history in Section~\ref{sec:r_corrections}: fixing the box-geometry inconsistency raised defect mAP@50 from 0.595 to 0.940, and that improvement was itself misleading, because it left the defect class separable by its capture conditions rather than by damage, a confound only exposed once the model was tested live rather than only on a held-out split drawn from the same source. Fixing that second problem is what actually resolved the live failure (Section~\ref{sec:r_live}), even though it lowered the offline number to 0.804. The general lesson is that a scarce class supplemented from an external source can pass every offline check while having learned the wrong signal, and that a corrective cycle which stops at re-training and re-scoring on a static split can leave exactly this fault in place; live or in-domain validation is not a superfluous final step but the only check in this study that caught it.
