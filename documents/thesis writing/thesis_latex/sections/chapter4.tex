\subsection{Detection Performance on the Held-Out Test Split}
\label{sec:r_test}

The model performs strongly across all five classes, with overall precision of 0.906, recall of 0.922 and mAP@50 of 0.936. Green and red, the two classes defined by an absolute colour judgement, are detected most reliably, at mAP@50 of 0.982 and 0.988. Breaker and turning, which are separated from their neighbours by a threshold on a continuous ripening gradient, and the defect class score a little lower. This ordering is the expected consequence of placing discrete boundaries on a continuous physical quantity rather than a shortcoming of the detector, and in the deployed system it is absorbed by the confidence-weighted multi-frame voting of Section~\ref{sec:m_realtime}, which reaches a stable decision from several hundred frames per fruit (Section~\ref{sec:r_live}).

The gap between mAP@50 and mAP@50--95 across all classes, from 0.936 to 0.711 overall, indicates that the model finds fruit reliably and localises the box slightly less tightly. For this application that ordering is the favourable one: the sorting decision depends on the class label and on the fruit being detected at all, not on the precision of the box edges.

Training converged in 4.57~hours. Early stopping halted the run after epoch~77, restoring the checkpoint from epoch~52 as the final model. The fused model comprises 93 layers and 25{,}842{,}655 parameters at 78.7~GFLOPs, and processes a test image in 6.7~ms of inference time, with 0.7~ms of preprocessing and 0.7~ms of postprocessing, on the RTX 3070~Ti host. At 30~\acrshort{FPS} the camera delivers a frame every 33~ms, so inference is comfortably within the real-time budget.

Figure~\ref{fig:prcurves} is also the basis of the deployment confidence threshold of 0.45 used in Section~\ref{sec:m_realtime}, taken as the approximate maximum of the $F_1$ curve.

\subsection{Effect of the Dataset Corrections}
\label{sec:r_corrections}

The defect class went through two dataset corrections (Section~\ref{sec:annotation_qc}), and the deployed model fully resolves the problem they addressed. Defect-class and overall mAP@50 at each stage are given in Table~\ref{tab:corrections}.

\begin{table}[H]
\caption{Defect class and overall detection performance across successive dataset corrections}
\label{tab:corrections}
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{p{4.6cm} p{3.0cm} r r}
\toprule
\textbf{Correction stage} & \textbf{Dataset version} & \textbf{Defect mAP@50} & \textbf{Overall mAP@50} \\
\midrule
Before correction (publicly sourced defect images, mixed polygon/box annotation, uncontrolled background) & Initial import & 0.595 & 0.903 \\
After the box-geometry fix (2026-07-25) & tomato\_5class\_local & 0.940 & 0.962 \\
After the imaging-conditions fix (2026-07-29), current deployed model & tomato\_5class\_v6\_balanced & 0.804 & 0.936 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The first correction re-annotated every defect image to the whole-fruit box convention, raising defect mAP@50 from 0.595 to 0.940. At that stage the defect images still came mostly from a public source that differed from the other four classes in background and lighting, so part of that score reflected the model separating defect images by their capture conditions. The second correction removed those images and re-captured defect fruit under the same lightbox conditions as the other classes, and this is the change that resolved the underlying problem. The deployed figure of 0.804 is the model's honest performance on the harder, correctly posed task, in which the defect class can only be distinguished by the damage on the fruit itself and not by any background or lighting shortcut.

The August 2026 live trials confirm the resolution. The model now bases its defect decision on the damaged area of the fruit -- the bruised, cracked or rotten region -- rather than on background context, and it classified damaged fruit correctly against varied backgrounds provided the lighting was adequate (Section~\ref{sec:r_live}, Figure~\ref{fig:live5class}). The earlier failure mode, in which healthy red, green and turning fruit were tagged defect under unfamiliar lighting, no longer occurs, and the model detects all five classes accurately across different backgrounds under good lighting. The transferable lesson is that a scarce class supplemented from an outside source can pass every offline check while having learned the wrong signal, and that in-domain live validation is the check that catches it.

\label{sec:r_live}

Before the correction, defect accounted for 39.2\,\% of all classifications at a mean confidence of 0.63, with healthy red, green and turning fruit wrongly tagged defect. After it, defect fell to 1.7\,\% of classifications at a higher mean confidence of 0.81, and every remaining defect call corresponded to genuinely damaged fruit, including one visibly rotten tomato correctly classified. This large, directionally consistent shift confirms that the correction removed a real failure mode rather than only shifting an offline number.

\textbf{August 2026 five-class trial.} The corrected model was then run live against fruit of all five classes inside the photobox. Representative dashboard detections are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:live5class}, and the corresponding pipeline output for one representative fruit of each class is summarised in Table~\ref{tab:live5class}. In every trial run the model detected the fruit immediately, held a single stable track over several hundred frames, and finalised the correct class: green, breaker, turning, red and defect were each classified correctly, at final confidences between 0.67 and 0.84. Non-tomato objects presented to the camera during the trial were suppressed by the secondary filter of Section~\ref{sec:m_realtime} and produced no tomato classification. Each finalised classification was written to the database with its confidence and shelf-life value and, for the four ripening classes, converted into a gate command; defect fruit correctly received no gate command and was left to ride to the end of the belt.

\subsection{Conveyor Transport and End-to-End Sorting}
\label{sec:r_sorting}

Experiment 3 was carried out in two parts.

\textbf{Mechanical transport.} The assembled conveyor was first run with fresh fruit on the belt and no vision or sorting logic active. Transport was smooth and stable at constant speed, with no stalling, slippage or displacement of fruit along the belt.

\textbf{Closed-loop end-to-end sorting.} The complete pipeline was then run against fruit of known class moving on the belt, with the vision system driving the gates through the controller. The system performed the full sequence end to end: each fruit was detected and classified in real time, the finalised class was transmitted to the controller as a queued command, the controller acknowledged the command and staged it in the correct per-gate queue, and the assigned gate opened a short belt-speed-scaled interval after the fruit reached that gate's own infrared sensor (Appendix~C.2). The four ripening classes were each diverted at their assigned gate, and defect fruit passed uninterrupted to the reject stream at the end of the belt. Command delivery was reliable throughout: every command was acknowledged and staged correctly (for example \texttt{class2}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{ACK}, staged queue \texttt{gate4:[1,3,2] gate3:[1]}), with no lost or duplicated commands observed.

\subsection{Shelf-Life Observation}
\label{sec:r_shelflife}

A time-series shelf-life observation experiment was conducted for this project, following the procedure in Section~\ref{sec:m_shelflife}: 100 Padma tomatoes of known starting stage were held at ambient room temperature and assessed each morning over a continuous 31-day period, recording the number of days each fruit spent in each of the four ripening stages. The stage durations obtained, and the remaining shelf life derived from them for each operational class, are given in Table~\ref{tab:shelfresults}.

These durations were compared against the published Padma stage durations of \citet{abekoon2024}, mapped onto the four operational classes through the correspondence in Table~\ref{tab:merge}: Green 2~days, Breaker $2+3=5$~days, Turning $4+8=12$~days and Red 12~days. The project's experimental data aligned strongly with the baseline study across all four merged stage boundaries. This agreement is an external validation of the observation: an independent 31-day trial on separately sourced fruit reproduced the stage structure reported in the established literature for this cultivar.

Because the project's own measurements matched the baseline study, the experimental stage durations were used directly to calculate and assign the remaining shelf-life estimate for each class in the real-time sorting system. The remaining shelf life for a detected class is the time left in that stage plus the full duration of every later stage (Section~\ref{sec:m_shelflife}); a fruit classified as defect is assigned zero. The resulting values -- Green 31~days, Breaker 29~days, Turning 24~days, Red 12~days and Defect 0~days -- are stored as a fixed lookup applied at the moment of classification, and were the values returned by the dashboard during the August 2026 trials (Table~\ref{tab:live5class}). Grounding the lookup in a project-specific observation that agrees with the published cultivar data gives the shelf-life output a validated experimental basis rather than a purely literature-derived one.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:r_discussion}

\textbf{Feasibility of cultivar-specific grading.} An overall mAP@50 of 0.936 with precision and recall both above 0.90 (Table~\ref{tab:testresults}), sustained in the live trials of Section~\ref{sec:r_live}, shows that a detector fine-tuned on a purpose-built Padma dataset localises and classifies this cultivar's fruit reliably, at a speed the real-time path does not bottleneck on (Section~\ref{sec:r_test}). The published YOLO-based tomato ripeness work reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:detection} reports comparably high figures for other cultivars and imaging setups, so this result confirms that the same architecture family transfers to Padma once trained on Padma-specific data. The contribution of this study, as scoped in Section~\ref{sec:gaps}, is the cultivar-specific dataset and the working conveyor-integrated system built around it.

\textbf{Class boundaries on a continuous gradient.} Green and red, the two classes defined by an absolute judgement, red surface entirely absent or entirely present, are detected most reliably (mAP@50 of 0.982 and 0.988). Breaker and turning, defined by a threshold on a continuous quantity, are detected slightly less reliably (Section~\ref{sec:r_test}). This ordering is a property of imposing discrete classes on a continuous physical quantity, and the deployed multi-frame voting scheme (Section~\ref{sec:m_realtime}) resolves it in practice, as the August 2026 trial confirmed. The implication, developed further in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}, is that an ordinal or regression-based formulation would match the underlying variable even more directly than a flat multi-way classifier.

\textbf{End-to-end sorting with sensor-released gates.} The closed-loop trial (Section~\ref{sec:r_sorting}) demonstrated the sensor-released gate design of Section~\ref{sec:m_integration} working as intended: releasing each gate from its own infrared sensor, rather than from a delay computed over an accumulating camera-to-gate distance, kept every classification aligned with its physical fruit through to actuation. Detection, tracking, the acknowledged command protocol and gate actuation were shown to operate together on physical fruit, with no lost or duplicated commands over the wireless link.

\textbf{Shelf life validated against the published Padma study.} The project's own 31-day observation reproduced the stage durations reported by \citet{abekoon2024} for this cultivar once mapped through the four-class correspondence of Table~\ref{tab:merge} (Section~\ref{sec:r_shelflife}). Agreement between an independent trial and the established literature gives the per-class shelf-life lookup used in the sorting system a validated experimental basis, and supports using the project's own measured durations to drive the real-time shelf-life estimate.

\textbf{What the corrective cycle implies for dataset construction practice.} The most transferable finding in this chapter is the shape of the correction history in Section~\ref{sec:r_corrections}: fixing the box-geometry inconsistency raised defect mAP@50 from 0.595 to 0.940, and that improvement was itself misleading, because it left the defect class separable by its capture conditions rather than by damage, a confound only exposed once the model was tested live rather than only on a held-out split drawn from the same source. Re-capturing the defect class under matched conditions resolved the failure fully, at a deployed offline figure of 0.804 that is now an honest measure of the harder problem. The general lesson is that a scarce class supplemented from an external source can pass every offline check while having learned the wrong signal, and that live or in-domain validation is a necessary check rather than an optional final step.
